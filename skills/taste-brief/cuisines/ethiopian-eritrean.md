## Hard-filter terms (mandatory)
- **Pork** is essentially never served: both Orthodox Christians and Muslims avoid it. Pork-free does not mean halal.
- **Halal**: Orthodox Christian and Muslim slaughter are separate traditions, and many Orthodox eaters also avoid meat slaughtered by Muslims. Never infer either one. Ask: 'ሥጋው ሐላል ነው?' (sigaw halal new? = is the meat halal?).
- **Fasting food (ye-tsom / nay tsom; beyaynetu combo)**: true fasting food is cooked in oil only, with no niter kibbeh, no ayib, no eggs and no meat. Some kitchens abroad finish the 'vegan' combo with butter, so say so: 'የጾም — በዘይት ብቻ፣ ያለ ቅቤ' (ye-tsom, be-zeyt bicha, yale kibe = fasting food, oil only, without butter). Fasting days are Wednesdays, Fridays and the long fasts such as Lent (Abiy Tsom / Tsome Hudade). Fish is allowed on many fasting days but not all, and practice varies, so don't assume.
- **Raw meat**: kitfo **tere** and **gored gored** are raw beef. Gloss them for anyone who hasn't ordered them before.
- **Organ meat**: **dulet** is minced tripe, liver and beef; **kwanta** is dried beef. Gloss both.
- **Lamb / goat**: 'beg' = lamb and 'fiyel' = goat. A 'key wat' may be beef or lamb, so name the meat.
- **Gluten**: injera abroad is often cut with wheat or barley. A gluten-free eater needs 100% teff, confirmed.
- **Dairy and egg**: ayib comes with kitfo; doro wat comes with a hard-boiled egg.
- **Alcohol**: tej (honey wine) and tella (home beer) are alcoholic.

## Home vs restaurant fidelity markers
- **Injera**: at home it's mostly or entirely teff (blends with barley, sorghum or wheat are also common in Ethiopia and Eritrea), sour and spongy. Abroad it's often teff cut with wheat, barley or self-rising flour (sometimes leavened with club soda), so it's paler and less sour. Ask for '100% teff if you have it'. It's often a surcharge or a separate item, and it matters for gluten-free eaters.
- **Kitfo** doneness is ordered by name: **tere** (raw), **leb leb** (lightly warmed), **yebesele** (cooked). At home leb leb or tere is usual; kitchens abroad default to cooked for non-Ethiopians. Name the doneness explicitly.
- **Heat**: at home, berbere is cooked into wats at the cook's usual level. Kitfo has mitmita mixed in, with more on the side; tibs and gored gored come with awaze (berbere paste) on the side. Abroad, kitchens often tone the berbere and mitmita down for outsiders. The default request is the home level: 'as you normally make it, with mitmita mixed in, awaze on the side.' Ask for more mitmita or berbere only on the eater's word: 'ሚጥሚጣ ይብዛ' (mitmita yibza = more mitmita). Side request: 'አዋዜ ለብቻ' (awaze lebicha = awaze separately).
- **Doro wat**: at home it's dark and built on long-cooked onion, with a hard-boiled egg and a chicken leg. Quick export versions are thin and pale.
- **Sweetness**: nothing in the savory core is sweet at home. A sugar request is rarely needed; use 'no sugar' only if a sauce tastes sweetened.

## Export drift (generic; no named markets)
Wheat-blend injera, kitfo cooked through by default, milder berbere and mitmita, and butter slipped into 'vegan' fasting combos.

## Insider vocabulary (for chef notes)
**Language routing:** an Eritrean or Tigrayan kitchen gets Tigrinya or plain English, never Amharic. Other Ethiopian kitchens (Amhara, Addis, Gurage) get Amharic. If the kitchen's origin is unclear, use English with dish names only. Never call an Eritrean place 'Ethiopian' in a note.

Amharic and Tigrinya phrasing is less verified here. Keep the native line short and let the English line carry the meaning.

**Address default:** no address term, or 'Chef'. Amharic እባክዎ (ebakwo) is the honorific, gender-neutral 'please'; avoid the gendered እባክህ/እባክሽ. In Tigrinya, the plural በጃኹም (bejakhum) works as a respectful, neutral 'please'. Etiye/Gashe (older woman/man) are warm but assume age and gender; skip them in written notes.

The highest-signal move is using the dish's own vocabulary correctly: tere, leb leb, mitmita, ayib, ye-tsom. That reads as an insider without needing full sentences.

## Meal composition norms
Meals are eaten communally on a shared injera platter. Per 2 people: 2–3 wats, or a beyaynetu combo, plus extra injera rolls. Kitfo comes with ayib and gomen.

## In-culture tells (market-agnostic; market specifics live in `kitchen-scout/markets/`)
Abroad, the kitchens are almost always in-culture. Quality differences come down to teff purity (100% teff injera offered) and the kitfo doneness default.

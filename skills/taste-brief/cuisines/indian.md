## Home vs restaurant fidelity markers
- **Paneer bhurji**: home = DRY scramble (onion, tomato, green chili, haldi), heat ~4, no sugar. Restaurants abroad often serve it in gravy. Read the menu description and require "dry, no gravy" if needed.
- **Egg bhurji**: home = dry, green-chili-forward (heat ~4–5), with pav or roti.
- **Butter chicken / paneer makhani**: home/Delhi versions are tangy-buttery and only mildly sweet (sweet low, heat ~3). Export versions are dessert-sweet and cream-heavy. Ask for less, not none: "kam meetha, cream kam" (कम मीठा, क्रीम कम). Use "no sugar" only for dishes with none at home (bhurji, dry sabzi, Kolhapuri).
- **Gujarati dal / kadhi**: home = sweet-sour by design. Don't ask for no sugar; ask for it as usually made.
- **Bengali curries**: many carry a pinch of sugar at home. If an export version tastes sweeter, ask for "a little less sweet", not none.
- **Vindaloo**: home (Goan) = vinegar-garlic marinade, Kashmiri chilies, sour-hot (heat ~6), traditionally pork, no potato (the name comes from vinha d'alhos). Export = generic hottest-on-menu curry with potato. Must: "vinegar-forward, no potato, heat as in Goa". Pork only if the eater has confirmed they eat it.
- **Naan**: an Indian tandoor naan is thin-ish with charred spots. Many export kitchens serve huge, sweet, fluffy naan. Specify it.
- **Daily breads**: daily home bread is roti/chapati/phulka, not naan. Homecoming eaters usually want roti as the base.
- **Dal**: home dal is a thin, tempered (tadka) dal. "Dal makhani" is restaurant-rich. Don't substitute one for the other.
- **Biryani**: the regional styles are not interchangeable (Hyderabadi dum, Lucknowi, Kolkata with potato, Malabar). Name which.
- **Chicken 65** (menus sometimes riff on the name): Chennai bar snack, dry, deep-fried with curry leaf and red chili, heat ~6. Export versions are often saucy or sweet; ask for it dry.

## Export drift (generic; no named markets)
Kitchens cooking for outsiders push North Indian gravies sweeter and creamier, serve sweeter and larger naan, add gravy to dry dishes, swap regional dishes for the standard butter chicken / tikka list, and either mute heat across the board or make "spicy" a blunt chili-powder add-on rather than the dish's own spice balance.

## Insider vocabulary (for chef notes)
**Language routing:** pick the language from the kitchen's provenance, not the "Indian" label. Hindi must not stand in for Tamil, Malayalam, Telugu, Kannada, Bengali or Marathi kitchens; Tamil/Hindi is a sensitive pair. Muslim-run Mughlai, Awadhi or Hyderabadi kitchens may prefer Urdu register ("shukriya", "Assalamu alaikum") — use it only when provenance says so. When unsure: English plus dish names, optionally plus the regional language.

**Address default:** no address term, or "Hi Chef / kitchen team", or "Namaste ji" (ji is respectful and gender-neutral). Only when the cook's gender is known: Bhaiya ji (भैया जी, North; avoid in Maharashtra) / Didi (दीदी); Dada / Didi (Bengal; दादा also Marathi); Anna / Akka (Tamil, Telugu); Chetta / Chechi (Kerala). Never use terms that mark the cook as junior (e.g., chhotu, thambi).

## Meal composition norms
Per person: about 1 main plus a share of a second, 2–3 rotis or 1 rice, and something cooling or raw (onion salad, raita, chutney). For 2 people, 3–4 dishes need about 5 breads plus rice if there's a gravy dish. Dry dishes need bread; gravy dishes want rice too.

## In-culture tells (market-agnostic; market specifics live in `kitchen-scout/markets/`)
- Regional dishes beyond the standard butter chicken / tikka / naan list (misal, Kolhapuri, gongura, appam).
- Indian-language reviewers praising "ghar jaisa".
- Community ties (embassy catering, temple events) and owner names or press coverage.

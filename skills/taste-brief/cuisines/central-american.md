# Central American: profile

**Central American food is not Mexican food.** It shares corn, beans and the word "tamal", but the dishes, the tortillas, the salsas and the heat are different. Don't route a Salvadoran, Guatemalan or Honduran request to `mexican.md`, and don't treat a "Mexican & Salvadoran" menu's Mexican half as part of the brief. Each country is its own tradition; never blend them into one "Latin" plate.

Belize leans Caribbean (rice and beans, stew chicken, Creole and Garifuna food); route it to `caribbean.md` unless the eater means Garifuna or Maya dishes covered here.

**Native defaults:** the plate is almost always mild. Heat lives in a bottle, a jar of curtido or chilero, or a small dish of chile on the table, so it is listed under `side`. "Authentic" here means un-Mexicanized and un-adapted, not spicy.

## Hard-filter terms (mandatory)
Never infer these. Ask in Spanish; confirm in the menu description when possible.
- **Pork and lard are common, often unnamed:** chicharrón (in pupusas revueltas, ground to a paste; on yuca and vigorón), nacatamal (pork plus lard masa), tamales, refried and volteado beans (often lard). Ask: "¿Lleva carne de cerdo, chicharrón o manteca de cerdo?"
- **"Vegetarian" pupusas:** frijol con queso and queso con loroco have no meat, but the beans may carry lard and the griddle is shared with chicharrón. Ask: "Es para una persona vegetariana: ¿los frijoles llevan manteca de cerdo?"
- **Chicken broth:** rice (arroz con pollo aside, plain rice too), sopas and some bean pots use caldo de pollo or consomé cubes. Ask: "¿El arroz o los frijoles llevan caldo de pollo?"
- **Pork-free ≠ halal.** Halal-slaughtered meat is rare in these kitchens; filter at kitchen-scout, not in a note. If asking: "¿La carne es halal?"
- **Shellfish:** sopa de caracol is conch (a mollusk, often with shrimp or crab); tapado and rondón mix fish and shellfish; ceviche may contain shrimp. Ask: "¿Lleva mariscos?"
- **Dairy, with a false friend:** in Honduras "mantequilla" usually means a cultured cream, not butter; Salvadorans say "crema" (whether "mantequilla" is also used this way in El Salvador is unverified). Baleadas, plato típico and pupusas carry cheese and crema. Ask: "Sin queso ni crema (mantequilla), por favor."
- **Meats many eaters don't expect (gloss them):** mondongo (tripe), sopa de pata (cow's foot and tripe), hilachas (shredded beef), chumpe (turkey), gallina (stewing hen, tougher and richer than chicken), moronga (blood sausage), lengua (tongue), garrobo (iguana, rare on menus).
- **Holy Week and Christmas:** seafood and dried fish appear in Semana Santa; tamales are seasonal or weekend-only in many kitchens. Check the menu, don't assume.

## Home vs restaurant fidelity markers
- **Pupusas**: home = thick, griddled, fully sealed masa rounds, eaten by hand, with vinegary curtido and thin cooked tomato salsa on the side. Rice-flour pupusas (de arroz, from Olocuilta) are a real variant; name which. Export = thin, oversized, under-filled, or served with Mexican salsa and sour cream. Ask for curtido and salsa roja aparte.
- **Curtido**: home = cabbage, carrot, onion, oregano and vinegar, lightly fermented; sour, not creamy. Never coleslaw.
- **Tamales**: Salvadoran tamales de gallina (soft, in plantain leaf), Guatemalan tamales colorados (recado, plantain leaf) and chuchitos (small, firm, in corn husk), Nicaraguan nacatamales (large, pork, rice, potato, mint, plantain leaf) and Costa Rican Christmas tamales are all different from Mexican tamales and from each other. Name which.
- **Gallo pinto**: Nicaraguan = red beans, fried with rice and onion; Costa Rican = black beans, often with Lizano and culantro. Both countries claim it; never tell a cook one is the other's.
- **Baleada**: home = a thick, soft flour tortilla folded over refried red beans, mantequilla and queso duro; "con todo" adds egg, avocado or meat. Export may use a thin Mexican flour tortilla or black beans.
- **Pepián / jocón / kak'ik**: home = recado sauces thickened with toasted seeds and tortilla or bread, mild to moderate; kak'ik is a red turkey broth with its own chile cobanero heat (Q'eqchi' dish, Cobán). Export may serve them as a generic "mole"; they are not mole. Everyday pepián and jocón are savory, not sweet; a few versions (pepián dulce, some pepián negro) add chocolate and are sweeter, so name which and don't ask for sugar to be removed from those.
- **Vigorón**: home = boiled yuca, chicharrón and sour cabbage-tomato salad on a banana leaf; the sour salad is the point.
- **Casado**: home = rice, black beans, picadillo or salad, maduro, and a protein on one plate, with Lizano on the table. Don't let it become a burrito bowl.
- **Sancocho (Panamá)**: home = clear hen soup with ñame and culantro, rice on the side, hot sauce only if the eater adds it. Not the Dominican or Colombian sancocho; name which.
- **Tortillas**: Central American corn tortillas are smaller and thicker than Mexican; Honduran baleada and Panamanian tortilla frita are different breads again.

## Export drift (generic; no named markets)
Kitchens cooking for outsiders, or sharing a menu with Mexican food, swap in Mexican items and salsas, add jalapeño, chipotle or pico de gallo, put yellow cheese and sour cream on everything, use thin Mexican tortillas, make curtido creamy, serve tamales as Mexican husk tamales, and call recado dishes "mole". The correction is back to mild plates with the native side condiments, not up to Mexican heat.

## Insider vocabulary (for chef notes)
**Language routing:** write notes in Central American Spanish with usted, which is polite and safe everywhere. Salvadorans, Guatemalans, Hondurans, Nicaraguans and Costa Ricans speak with voseo (vos), and Costa Ricans use usted even with family; Panamanians mostly use tú/usted. A note never needs vos; usted is never wrong. Avoid Mexican-only words: write "picante" or "chile" for heat (not "picoso"), "salsa picante" or the local name for the side heat (chilero in Nicaragua and Costa Rica, ají in Panamá, chile/picante elsewhere). K'iche', Q'eqchi', Kaqchikel and Garifuna are home languages for many cooks; use them only for dish names (kak'ik, hudutu, tapou), never for the note. Fallback: English plus dish names.

**Sensitivities:** never call the food or the cook Mexican. Don't merge countries ("Salvadoran-Honduran style"). National nicknames (guanaco, chapín, catracho, nica, tico) are self-used and friendly, but leave them out of notes; name the country instead.

**Address default:** "Hola, buenas." (or "Buenas tardes/noches") in usted, with no form of address. Avoid "jefe/jefa" (gendered), "joven", "muchacho/a", "mija/mijo" (marks the cook as junior or is too familiar).

## Meal composition norms
2–3 pupusas per person (curtido and salsa are mandatory, not optional); 1–2 baleadas per person; plato típico, casado and sancocho are each a full plate for one. Tamales and nacatamales are one large or two small per person, often a weekend or holiday meal with bread or coffee. Every table has its condiment: curtido, encurtido, chilero, chirmol or ají. Drinks matter: horchata de morro, refresco de cebada, pinolillo, cacao, chicha.

## In-culture tells (market-agnostic; market specifics live in `kitchen-scout/markets/`)
- A country named on the menu, not "Latin" or "Hispanic"; dishes that only that country makes (loroco, pepián, baleadas con mantequilla, vigorón, casado, sancocho with ñame).
- Pupusas made to order on a griddle, with curtido in a jar.
- Weekend-only tamales or nacatamales; seasonal Semana Santa or Christmas specials.
- Spanish reviews using voseo or local words (pupusería, comedor, fritanga, soda).
- A shared Mexican menu is not disqualifying, but judge the kitchen on its own-country dishes only.

## Hard-filter terms (mandatory)
- **Halal ≠ pork-free.** Mindanao Muslim cooking is halal and pork-free; a kitchen that also serves pork is not a faithful source for it. Most other Filipino cooking assumes pork. Ask: "Halal po ba ito?" (Is this halal?) / "Wala po bang baboy ito?" (Is there no pork in this?)
- **Lard and pork fat.** Frying is often in lard (mantika ng baboy) or pork fat; chicharon is a common topping. Ask: "May baboy o mantika ng baboy po ba ito?"
- **Fish sauce and shrimp paste in "vegetable" dishes.** Pinakbet, dinengdeng, laing, ginataang gulay and most vegetable stews use bagoong (fermented fish or shrimp) or patis (fish sauce), and often pork or dried shrimp too. Kare-kare comes with bagoong on the side. Ask: "Gulay lang po ba ito? Walang karne, patis, o bagoong po?"
- **Meats many eaters don't expect (gloss them):** dinuguan = pork blood stew; sisig = chopped pig face and ears (sometimes chicken liver); bopis = pork lung and heart; papaitan = goat or beef offal with bile; kare-kare = oxtail and tripe; isaw = grilled intestine; betamax = grilled blood cubes; balut = fertilized duck egg; kinunot = stingray or shark.
- **Fasting:** many Catholic eaters skip meat on Lenten Fridays and Good Friday (fish is fine). Muslim eaters fast during Ramadan; order for after sunset.

## Home vs restaurant fidelity markers
- **Filipino sweetness is often by design.** At home, spaghetti, tocino, longganisa hamonado and pork BBQ skewers are sweet. Don't ask to "fix" these; if the eater wants, ask for "less sweet", not "no sugar". Exception: Vigan, Lucban and Alaminos longganisa are garlicky and sour (de recado), not sweet. Name the style.
- **Sinigang**: home = properly sour (maasim); export = tamed. Ask for it sour the way it is made at home.
- **Adobo** varies by region and household (soy vs white adobo, with or without coconut). Ask the eater which.
- **Sisig**: home = sizzling, with crispy bits, calamansi and chili; the mayo-heavy versions are newer and mainstream, not Pampanga-original.
- **Sawsawan is dish-specific**: Tagalog lechon = liver sauce (sarsa ng lechon); Cebu lechon = none or spiced vinegar; bagnet = sukang Iloko + KBL/bagoong; inasal = sinamak + calamansi + soy + chicken oil; fried fish = suka't bawang. Ask for extra sawsawan without overriding the type.
- **Heat** stays at the native default above. Raise it only on the eater's word.

## Export drift (generic; no named markets)
Kitchens cooking for outsiders tame the sour (sinigang, paksiw, kinilaw), cut fermented funk (bagoong, patis, burong isda), swap offal and pork fat for leaner cuts (chicken sisig, no chicharon bits), add mayo or cheese, and drop the dish-specific sawsawan. Undo each with a `must:` line; don't add sweetness corrections to dishes that are sweet at home.

## Insider vocabulary (for chef notes)
**Language routing:** Tagalog (Filipino) is the national lingua franca and the default. Cooks from the Visayas and Mindanao may speak Cebuano (Bisaya), Hiligaynon (Ilonggo) or Tausug at home, and Ilocano and Kapampangan are distinct languages too. Tagalog is understood almost everywhere and is not offensive, but if the kitchen's provenance is clearly Visayan or Mindanaoan, English plus dish names is an equally good choice. "Po" is Tagalog-specific; it does not carry over to Cebuano.

**Address default:** no term is needed; "po" carries the respect. Kuya (m) / Ate (f) only when the cook's gender is known. "Chef" is a safe neutral.

## Meal composition norms
Rice is mandatory, about 1.5 cups per person (garlic rice for breakfast-style "silog" plates). Shared ulam (mains), 2–3 for two people, mixing a soup (sinigang), a fried dish and a stew.

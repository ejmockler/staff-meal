## Hard-filter terms (mandatory)
- **Pork-free ≠ halal.** Pork is everywhere (kimchi jjigae, jeyuk, gukbap, samgyeopsal, many broths). Beef and chicken are not halal unless the kitchen says so, and marinades often contain cooking wine (맛술 matsul) or soju. Halal = 할랄 (hallal). Ask: "할랄 고기인가요?" (hallal gogi-ingayo?, is the meat halal?); for pork only: "돼지고기 빼 주세요" (dwaejigogi ppae juseyo).
- **Kimchi is not vegetarian by default.** It is usually made with jeotgal (salted shrimp 새우젓, anchovy sauce 멸치액젓). "Vegetable" jjigae and doenjang jjigae often use anchovy stock (멸치 육수). Ask: "젓갈이나 멸치 육수 들어가나요?" (jeotgal-ina myeolchi yuksu deureoganayo?, does it contain jeotgal or anchovy stock?).
- **Vegetarian / vegan:** "고기랑 해산물 빼 주세요" (gogirang haesanmul ppae juseyo, no meat or seafood). Temple food (사찰음식 sachal eumsik) also excludes the five pungent vegetables, garlic and green onion among them; don't assume a "vegan" dish meets this.
- **Wheat hides in sauces:** gochujang, soy sauce and many fry batters contain wheat. Gluten-free has to be confirmed, not inferred.
- **Unexpected meats (gloss them):** sundae (순대) = blood sausage in intestine casing; gopchang/makchang (곱창/막창) = small intestine / abomasum; hongeo (홍어) = fermented skate with a strong ammonia smell; sannakji (산낙지) = freshly cut live octopus, moving; beondegi (번데기) = silkworm pupae; seonji (선지) = coagulated ox blood in haejangguk.

## Home vs restaurant fidelity markers
- **Kimchi jjigae**: home = sour, well-aged kimchi (묵은지 mugeunji) with pork fat, sour med; export = fresh kimchi, tastes flat. Ask for well-ripened kimchi.
- **Tteokbokki**: home = sweet-spicy, heat about 4, sweet med (gochujang + sugar or mulyeot). Export drift = milder heat. Keep the sweetness; ask for native level, go hotter only on the eater's word, and ask for "덜 달게" (less sweet) only if the eater wants it.
- **Fried chicken**: yangnyeom is sweet at home (sweet high). Don't "fix" it; if the eater wants it, ask for less sweet, not none.
- **Gukbap**: home broth is mild; diners season it themselves with dadaegi, saeujeot and buchu. Ask for dadaegi on the side, not for it to be made spicy.
- **Banchan**: in Korea they are free and refillable. Abroad they're sparse or charged. Kimchi should be on the table.
- **Naengmyeon**: one name, two dishes. Name which: mul (cold broth, Pyongyang-style, heat 0) or bibim (sauced, Hamhung-style, heat 4).
- **Tangsuyuk**: two serving styles. Name which: bumeok (sauce poured over) or jjikmeok (sauce on the side for dipping).

## Export drift (generic; no named markets)
Marinades and glazes turned sweeter, kimchi served young instead of sour, stews milder, banchan reduced to one or two, menus collapsed to BBQ, fried chicken and bibimbap.

## Insider vocabulary (for chef notes)
**Language routing:** Korean for Korean kitchens, including Korean-Chinese (junghwa) kitchens, whose menus and working language are Korean. Ethnic-Korean cooks from China (Joseonjok) read Korean. Fallback: English plus dish names in Hangul.

**Address default:** open with 안녕하세요 alone, or add 사장님 (sajangnim, owner/boss: standard, warm, gender-neutral). Avoid 이모(님) and 아가씨 in written notes; they assume gender and age.

## Meal composition norms
One jjigae or main per person or to share, rice per person, and banchan. With fried chicken, add pickled radish (치킨무 chikin-mu). Pair BBQ with lettuce, ssamjang and garlic.

# Maghrebi (Moroccan, Algerian, Tunisian, Libyan): profile

Scope: the Maghreb. Mashriq, Egyptian, Gulf and Yemeni food → levantine-arab.md. Maghrebi Jewish home cooking (dafina/skhina, Tunisian bkaïla) shares many dishes but follows its own rules; see Hard filters.

## Traditions (never treat "North African" or the country as one cuisine)
Heat and sweetness split by country more than anywhere else in this profile: Tunisian food is chili-forward, Moroccan food is mild and often sweet-savory, Algerian and Libyan sit in between. Never carry one country's level to another.

Desserts and drinks across the region are sweet: high at home. That covers chebakia, sellou, makrout, zlabia, Maghrebi baklawa, Libyan asida with date syrup, and mint tea. Reduce only on the eater's word, and ask for "less", never "none".

## Hard-filter terms (mandatory)
Never infer these from the cuisine label or the kitchen's country. Ask, and record the answer in `dietary:` as confirmed.
- **Pork-free ≠ halal.** Pork is near-absent from Maghrebi menus, but that does not make the meat halal. Halal also concerns slaughter. French-style bistros and bars serving couscous and merguez, and kitchens that pour wine, are not automatically halal.
- **Kosher ≠ halal.** A Maghrebi Jewish kitchen may be kosher (closed on Shabbat, no meat-dairy mixing). Neither label stands in for the other; ask for the one the eater needs.
- **Merguez**: the home version is lamb, beef or a mix, with harissa, paprika and cumin, in sheep casing. Commercial merguez varies; ask what meat it is and whether it is halal.
- **Smen and butter in "vegetable" dishes**: smen (aged, fermented butter) and butter go into couscous, harira, rfissa, msemen and pastries. Ask for "bla smen" (without smen).
- **Meat broth in "vegetable" couscous**: the vegetable broth for couscous is usually cooked with meat or chicken, and so are harira, chorba and sharba. Ask.
- **Gluten**: couscous, frik, berkoukes, rechta, bazeen, msemen and warqa are all wheat or barley. Couscous is not a gluten-free grain.
- **Raw-ish egg**: Tunisian brik is meant to have a runny yolk. Flag it for anyone avoiding undercooked egg.
- **Tuna and fish in "salads"**: Tunisian mechouia, salade tunisienne, brik and fricassé usually contain tuna.
- **Meats many eaters don't expect (gloss them)**: pastilla with pigeon (hamam); usban / osbane (stuffed tripe sausage, Libya and Tunisia); douara (tripe and offal stew, Algeria); tête de mouton / raas (steamed or roast sheep's head) and mokh (brains) on mechoui and street menus; kalawi (kidneys); babbouche / ghlal (snails, Moroccan street food); camel at some Moroccan and Libyan kitchens.
- **Nuts**: almonds in pastilla, seffa, tagines with fruit and most pastries; peanuts in some sellou; sesame on khobz and chebakia.
- **Ramadan**: kitchens shift hours around ftour/iftar, and harira, chorba, chebakia and bourek become seasonal specials.

## Home vs restaurant fidelity markers
- **Couscous: one name, four dishes. Name which.** Moroccan: hand-rolled grain steamed two or three times in a couscoussier, seven vegetables, broth served alongside; tfaya adds sweet caramelized onion and raisins. Algerian: red (tomato) or white marqa, often chickpeas, with berkoukes as a larger-grain cousin. Tunisian: red tomato-harissa sauce worked into the grain, often fish on the coast. Libyan: lamb with chickpeas under basla onions. The export version is instant couscous boiled once with a stew spooned over it. `must:` steamed grain, broth as the region serves it.
- **Tagine: one name, two dishes.** Moroccan tagine is a slow-cooked stew in the conical pot. Tunisian tajine is a baked egg-and-cheese dish, closer to a frittata. Name which.
- **Moroccan sweetness is dish-specific.** At home, pastilla, mrouzia, tfaya and prune tagines are sweet-savory, while chicken with preserved lemon and olives, kefta with eggs and harira are not sweet. Export kitchens push honey and dried fruit into everything. Keep the sweet dishes sweet ("less sweet" only on the eater's word), and ask for no sugar only on dishes that are not sweet at home.
- **Pastilla**: home = warqa pastry, pigeon or chicken, almonds, egg, a cinnamon-sugar dusting on top. The sweet-savory contrast is native. If the eater wants it less sweet, ask for a lighter sugar dusting, not none. Seafood pastilla has no sugar.
- **Harissa**: in Tunisia it is in the dish and on the table. In Moroccan cooking it is a side condiment, not an ingredient of most tagines. Don't add harissa to a Moroccan dish by default, and don't mute a Tunisian one.
- **Preserved lemon**: home = salt-cured lemon (l'hamd markad), with its own fermented bitterness. Fresh lemon juice in its place is an export shortcut.
- **Harira vs chorba**: Moroccan harira is tomato, lentils and chickpeas thickened with flour (tedouira), finished with lemon and served with dates and chebakia. Algerian chorba frik is built on green cracked wheat. Libyan sharba uses orzo. They are not interchangeable.
- **Brik vs bourek**: Tunisian brik is a single malsouka sheet fried around an egg (yolk runs), tuna, capers and parsley. Algerian bourek is a rolled cigar of minced meat. Name which.
- **Mint tea**: home = green tea, fresh mint, a lot of sugar, poured from height for foam. All sweet levels are native; ask for less if the eater wants it.

## Export drift (generic; no named markets)
Instant couscous with the stew poured over, honey and dried fruit in every tagine, harissa added to Moroccan dishes or muted in Tunisian ones, fresh lemon for preserved lemon, no smen, generic "Moroccan spice" heavy on cinnamon, merguez as the default protein on every plate, and the menu blended into pan-"Mediterranean" with Levantine mezze.

## Insider vocabulary (for chef notes)
**Language routing:** pick from the kitchen's provenance, not the cuisine label.
- **Dialect, not Mashriqi Arabic.** Darija (Moroccan), Algerian Darja, Tunisian Derja and Libyan Arabic differ from Levantine and Egyptian. Don't reuse the levantine-arab.md rows (bidūn, bidd). Maghrebi cooks say "bla" for "without". Moroccan, Algerian and Tunisian dialects are broadly mutually intelligible; Libyan is closer to Tunisian and eastern Arabic.
- **Script.** Dialect is written both in Arabic script and in Latin "Arabizi" (3 = ع, 7 = ح, 9 = ق). Give both. Modern Standard Arabic is understood but reads stiff; use it only as a fallback.
- **French** is a working kitchen language for many Moroccan, Algerian and Tunisian cooks, and menus are often in French. Use French when the menu or provenance is French-leaning; use dialect when the menu shows Arabic script or dialect dish names. Use the polite "vous". For Libyan kitchens, French is not a default: use Libyan Arabic or English.
- **Amazigh / Tamazight.** Kabyle (Algeria), Tachelhit / Soussi, Tarifit and Central Atlas (Morocco) cooks may be Amazigh, not Arab. Never call an Amazigh kitchen "Arab". Write Arabic or French as the kitchen's menu does, and add the Tamazight greeting only when the kitchen signals Amazigh identity (Tifinagh signage, Kabyle or Soussi dishes).
- **Sensitive pairs:** Amazigh/Arabic (as above); Algerian/Moroccan (never label an Algerian dish or kitchen Moroccan or the reverse; couscous is a shared heritage, jointly inscribed by UNESCO in 2020 from a bid by Algeria, Mauritania, Morocco and Tunisia; harissa was inscribed for Tunisia in 2022). Fallback: English plus dish names in French spelling.

**Address default:** a plural greeting to the whole kitchen. Darija "llah y3awnkoum" (God help you all) and Algerian/Tunisian "ya3tikoum essa77a" (give you health) are neutral and warm. In French, "bon courage" is what people say to someone at work. Avoid "khouya" (my brother) and other male-assuming terms; "Chef" in French is fine. Never "wlidi" or "benti" (my son/daughter), which mark the cook as junior.

## Meal composition norms
The main dish is shared from one platter. One couscous or tagine feeds 2–3 people, with bread for every eater (about half a round khobz, or one kesra or tabouna, each) because bread is the utensil for tagines. Moroccan tables add 2–3 cooked salads (zaalouk, taktouka, carrot or beet). Tunisian meals often start with brik and salade mechouia or tunisienne, with harissa and olives on the table. Algerian and Libyan meals start with chorba or sharba, especially in Ramadan, then the main. Mint tea and a pastry close the meal. Bazeen is eaten by hand from a shared bowl.

## In-culture tells (market-agnostic; market specifics live in `kitchen-scout/markets/`)
- A menu in French and/or dialect with country-specific dishes (rfissa, tangia, chakhchoukha, rechta, lablabi, ojja, bazeen), not a generic "tagine and couscous".
- Hand-rolled, steamed couscous, often as a Friday special.
- Bread baked in-house (khobz, msemen, kesra, tabouna) and house-made merguez or harissa.
- Ramadan harira or chorba and chebakia specials.
- Halal is a filter, not a provenance signal; weight dialect dish names above it.

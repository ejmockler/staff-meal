## Hard-filter terms (mandatory)
Never infer these from the cuisine or the kitchen. Ask, and record the answer in `dietary:`.
- **Pork-free ≠ halal.** Halal also covers how the meat was slaughtered and alcohol in cooking (Shaoxing wine / ang chiew, mirin, arak). Pork and lard: *babi*, *minyak babi* (ID), *lemak babi* / *minyak babi* (MY). Chinese-Malaysian char kway teow is often fried in lard. Balinese, Manado, Batak and Chinese-Malaysian/Indonesian kitchens are often non-halal.
  - ID: "Mohon maaf, apakah menu ini halal? Apakah ada babi atau minyak babi?"
  - MY: "Maaf tanya, menu ini halal ke? Ada babi atau lemak babi tak?"
- **Shrimp paste and dried fish in "vegetable" dishes.** *Terasi* (ID) / *belacan* (MY) is in most sambals and in kangkung belacan. *Petis* (black shrimp paste) is in rujak cingur and lontong balap. Gado-gado, pecel and karedok sauces often contain terasi. *Ebi* / *udang kering* (dried shrimp), *ikan bilis* (anchovies, on nasi lemak), *budu* (anchovy sauce, East Coast).
  - ID: "Apakah sambal atau bumbunya pakai terasi, petis, atau ebi? Kalau bisa tanpa itu ya, terima kasih."
  - MY: "Sambal ni ada belacan atau udang kering tak? Kalau boleh tak nak letak ya, terima kasih."
- **Vegetarian.** Animal stock and shrimp paste hide in vegetable dishes. Ghee or margarine is used in roti canai and other Mamak breads. A Chinese-Malaysian "vegetarian" kitchen may be Buddhist (also no onion or garlic), which is fine for a vegetarian but not the same thing.
  - ID: "Saya vegetarian. Tolong tanpa daging, ikan, udang, terasi, dan kaldu hewani ya, terima kasih."
  - MY: "Saya makan sayur sahaja. Tolong tak nak daging, ikan, udang, belacan atau ikan bilis ya, terima kasih."
- **Unexpected meats (gloss them).** Minahasan menus: *RW* (rintek wuuk) = dog meat; *paniki* = fruit bat; *babi* = pork (babi rica, tinoransak). Cakalang fufu (smoked skipjack) and ayam/ikan rica-rica (chicken/fish) are safe defaults. Never order RW or paniki unless the eater asked for them by name. Offal and cuts across the region: *tunjang* = beef foot/tendon; *otak* = brain; *babat* = tripe; *paru* = lung; *usus* = intestine; *limpa* = spleen; *cingur* = cow snout (rujak cingur); *kikil* = cow-foot skin.
- **Fasting.** During Ramadan (*puasa*), Malay, Minang and Mamak kitchens may be closed by day or open only for buka puasa (sunset). Check hours, don't assume.

## Home vs restaurant fidelity markers
- **Rendang**: home (Minang) = dry, dark and caramelized, coconut cooked down to a paste. Export = a wet orange "rendang curry", which is closer to kalio or gulai. Ask for "rendang kering" (dry rendang). Malaysian rendang tok is also dry.
- **Sambal**: home = the dish's own sambal, never bottled sweet chili sauce. Raw sambals: matah (Bali), dabu-dabu (Manado). Cooked sambals: lado mudo/hijau (Padang, lightly cooked), balado. Sambal tumis on nasi lemak is sweet-spicy at home; keep it that way. If the eater wants more, ask for extra on the side (see vocabulary), not a hotter plate.
- **Javanese sweetness**: home = sweet. Gudeg, bacem and selat Solo are meant to be sweet. If the eater asks, request "kurang manis" (less sweet), never "tanpa gula" (no sugar).
- **Satay**: home = sweet peanut sauce and kecap-sweet marinade (sate Madura, satay Kajang). Sate Padang is different: a turmeric-rice-flour gravy, not peanut sauce.
- **Laksa**: Penang assam laksa (sour, tamarind, fish) and curry laksa (coconut) are different dishes. Name which.
- **Hokkien mee**: Penang = prawn mee soup; KL = dark-soy fried thick noodles. Name which.
- **Indisch rijsttafel** is a colonial-era Dutch-Indonesian banquet tradition. It is its own thing, not Indonesian home food.

## Export drift (generic; no named markets)
Kitchens cooking for outsiders tend to make rendang wet and saucy, swap house sambal for bottled sweet chili sauce, tone down Manado and Padang heat, and drop terasi/belacan and offal from the menu.

## Insider vocabulary (for chef notes)
**Language routing:** Indonesian (Bahasa Indonesia) for Indonesian kitchens, Malay (Bahasa Melayu) for Malaysian kitchens. They are close but not interchangeable: "banget", "nggak" and "kuah" phrasing read as Indonesian; "tak nak", "sikit" and "sangat" read as Malaysian. Regional cooks (Javanese, Sundanese, Minang, Manadonese) all read standard Indonesian, so use it rather than guessing a regional language. Chinese-Malaysian and Mamak counters: Malay or English, with dish names as written on the menu (Chinese characters where the menu uses them). At Chinese-Malaysian counters a short Mandarin line (加辣, 少甜, 干捞; rows below) is the cook's own language. At Mamak counters Malay works universally ("kuah banjir", "kurang manis"); a Tamil line is not needed. Both languages use Latin script, so the phrase is its own romanization.

**Address default:** no address, just "Halo / Hai, tolong ... ya, terima kasih". "Kak" (ID) is gender-neutral and respectful, and standard in app notes. Mas/Mbak (Javanese) and Abang/Kakak (MY) only when the cook's gender is known; Pak/Bu is formal. "Boss" is normal at Chinese and Mamak counters. Never "Dik/Adik" (marks the cook as junior).

## Meal composition norms
Rice-centered. Nasi Padang is rice plus 2–4 lauk (dishes) per person, chosen from the display. Satay is about 10 sticks per person as a meal (5 as a side), with lontong (ID) or nasi impit/ketupat plus cucumber and raw onion (MY), and peanut sauce. Sate Padang comes with its own gravy instead. Nasi lemak is a complete plate. Sambal and krupuk are on the table at most meals.

## In-culture tells (market-agnostic; market specifics live in `kitchen-scout/markets/`)
- Indonesian- or Malay-language reviews.
- Rendang kering.
- Regional specificity (Padang, Manado, Penang).
- Halal certification is a home-faithful signal for Malay, Padang/Minang, Mamak and Javanese kitchens. For Balinese, Manado, Batak and Chinese-Malaysian/Indonesian kitchens, a pork-forward menu (babi guling, babi rica, lard char kway teow) is the faithful signal.

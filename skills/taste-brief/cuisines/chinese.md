## Hard-filter terms (mandatory)
Never infer these from the cuisine or the restaurant name. Ask, in the cook's language.
- **Pork is the default meat.** Lard (猪油 zhūyóu) shows up in stir-fries, greens, pastries and some noodle soups. Pork stock is common in noodles and wontons. Claypot rice and fried rice often carry Chinese sausage (腊肠 làcháng, pork).
- **No pork ≠ halal.** Halal is 清真 (qīngzhēn), usually Hui or Uyghur kitchens. Han kitchens that "skip the pork" still use shared woks, lard and Shaoxing cooking wine (料酒 liàojiǔ, alcohol).
- **"Vegetarian" dishes may hide animal products:** oyster sauce (蚝油), fish sauce (鱼露), dried shrimp (虾米 / 虾皮), XO sauce (dried scallop, shrimp, ham), chicken stock or chicken powder (鸡汤 / 鸡精), lard. Mapo tofu normally contains minced beef or pork.
- **Buddhist vegetarian (斋 zhāi) is stricter:** no meat, fish or eggs, and often no "five pungents" (五辛: garlic, onion, scallion, leek, chives). Some eaters keep it only on the 1st and 15th of the lunar month.
- **Unexpected meats, gloss them for the eater:** 毛血旺 (duck blood, tripe, luncheon meat), 夫妻肺片 (beef offal), 肥肠 (pork intestine), 腰花 (pork kidney), 毛肚 / 百叶 (tripe), 鸭血 (duck blood), 牛蛙 (bullfrog), 鸡爪 / 凤爪 (chicken feet).
- **Allergen notes:** soy sauce contains wheat; kung pao and dan dan mian usually contain peanuts; sesame paste and sesame oil are common.

## Home vs restaurant fidelity markers
- **Mapo tofu**: home = real huajiao numbing, a red chili-oil sheen, doubanjiang depth, no sugar, and a light glossy glaze that clings to the tofu (some 勾芡 is correct). Export = mild, sweet, heavy gloopy slurry. Ask for 正宗川味 and "don't cut the huajiao" (花椒不要减量), not extra.
- **Kung pao (宫保鸡丁)**: home = light lychee-style (荔枝味) sweet-sour, sweet low, with dried chilies and huajiao. Export = heavy sweet brown sauce. Ask for "less sweet" (少放点糖), not "no sugar".
- **Gan bian (干煸)**: home = dry-fried, no sauce. If the menu shows a sauced version, note "干煸，不要汤汁".
- **Cantonese**: home = heat 0, light seasoning, a little sugar in many dishes (char siu is sweet). Don't add heat notes. Useful notes when the export version is over-seasoned: 要夠鑊氣 (plenty of wok hei); 唔好太鹹 (not too salty; Mandarin 不要太咸，清淡新鲜一点); 汁另上 (sauce on the side); 走葱 (no scallion). Use 少甜 (less sweet) for dishes sweet at home and 走糖 (no sugar) only for dishes with no sugar at home.
- **Shanghai hong shao rou**: home = sweet. The sweetness is authentic; don't "fix" it. Only on the eater's word, ask for less sweet (不要太甜), never no sugar.
- **Spice-level selector**: the mainland ladder is usually 微辣 / 中辣 / 重辣 / 特辣 (slight / medium / heavy / extra). Map the brief's heat: 4–5 → 微辣, 6 → 中辣, 7–8 → 重辣, 9+ → 特辣 (or the closest step the menu offers), and also write the note. The brief's heat comes from the native defaults above unless the eater stated otherwise, so 特辣 is never a default.

## Export drift (generic; no named markets)
Kitchens cooking for outsiders tend to add sugar and cornstarch, cut the huajiao and chili, swap offal and bone-in cuts for boneless meat, and collapse regional dishes into one generic "spicy" or "brown sauce" style. The notes restore the home level; they don't push past it.

## Insider vocabulary (for chef notes)
**Language routing:** pick the language from the kitchen's provenance, not the "Chinese" label.
- **Mainland-run kitchens:** Mandarin, simplified script.
- **Hong Kong / Cantonese-run kitchens:** Cantonese, traditional script, Jyutping romanization.
- **Taiwanese kitchens:** Mandarin in traditional script (滷肉飯, 牛肉麵, 三杯雞, 謝謝, 乾煸). Don't call the food "Chinese" in notes. Taiwanese spice ladder: 小辣 / 中辣 / 大辣.
- **Uyghur kitchens** (Uyghur-script menu, "Uyghur" in the name): write the note in English, don't call the food Chinese, and don't use Mandarin. A closing "Rehmet" (رەھمەت, thank you) is welcome. Mandarin is fine for kitchens that brand themselves 新疆 or 清真 in Chinese. Consider a standalone uyghur.md.

**Address default:** 师傅 / 師傅 (shīfu / si1 fu2) is the standard, gender-neutral respectful address for a cook. Otherwise no address term.

## Meal composition norms
Meals are shared: order one dish per person, plus a soup or a vegetable. Pair each fiery dish with a plain one (a stir-fried green or a steamed dish). Rice comes per person. Northern meals may use dumplings or noodles instead of rice.

## In-culture tells (market-agnostic; market specifics live in `kitchen-scout/markets/`)
- Regional names (四川/湖南/东北/新疆), a separate "traditional" or Chinese-language menu, and Chinese-only reviews.
- A Chinese-language review that says "很正宗" (very authentic) is the strongest single signal.

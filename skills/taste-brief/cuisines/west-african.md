## Hard-filter terms (mandatory)
- **Pork**: rare on Nigerian, Ghanaian and Senegalese menus, but it exists (pork pepper soup, Ghanaian pork khebab). Pork-free does not mean halal. Northern Nigerian and Senegalese food is cooked by largely Muslim communities, yet a kitchen abroad may not use halal meat. Never infer it. Ask: Pidgin 'Abeg, the meat na halal?'; Hausa 'Don Allah, naman halal ne?'; French 'La viande est-elle halal, s'il vous plaît ?'.
- **Fish and crayfish in "vegetable" soups**: efo riro, egusi, okra, banga, afang and edikang ikong are normally cooked with ground crayfish, stockfish, dried fish and meat stock. Shito contains dried fish or shrimp. A vegetarian or pescatarian request must say so: Pidgin 'Abeg, no put crayfish, stockfish, fish or meat stock inside.'
- **Bouillon cubes** (Maggi, Knorr) are in almost everything. Ask if the eater avoids MSG or animal-based stock.
- **Unexpected meats** (gloss them): shaki (tripe), ponmo/kpomo (cow skin), "assorted" (mixed offal), nkwobi (cow foot), isi ewu (goat head), igbin (snail), periwinkle, bushmeat/grasscutter (cane rat). Asun is goat. "Meat" on a menu often means beef or goat, not chicken.
- **Peanut allergy**: yaji on suya and kilishi, groundnut soup, mafé, miyan taushe, kuli-kuli. Egusi is melon seed, not a nut.
- **Gluten**: most swallows (pounded yam, eba, fufu, amala, banku) are gluten-free, but semovita, "wheat" swallow and some instant fufu mixes are not.
- **Palm oil**: central to most Nigerian soups. It can't be swapped without changing the dish.

## Home vs restaurant fidelity markers
- **Jollof** is regional. Nigerian home jollof uses long-grain parboiled rice and tastes smoky; "party jollof" has bottom-pot char. Ghanaian uses non-parboiled aromatic rice (jasmine/'perfumed' or basmati), is more aromatic, and usually comes with shito. Name which one. Export versions can be pale, wet or sweetened with extra tomato paste; ask for the home style, not for more heat.
- **Heat**: at home, scotch bonnet is cooked in at the family's usual level, which is clearly hot for Yoruba, Igbo and Delta stews and pepper soup, moderate for jollof and Efik soups, and mild in Senegalese food, where pepper sauce goes on the side. Export kitchens may tone it down for outsiders. The default request is the home level: Pidgin 'Abeg, cook am the way una dey cook am for house, no reduce the pepper.' For Ghanaian kitchens, ask for 'shito on the side' with jollof and waakye; for Senegalese kitchens, 'piment à côté' (pepper sauce on the side). Ask for more pepper only on the eater's word.
- **Sweetness**: savory stews are not sweet at home. Plantain (dodo, kelewele) and yassa onions are naturally sweet; leave them. Use 'no sugar' only if a stew tastes sweetened.
- **Swallow pairing**: egusi with pounded yam or eba, ewedu with amala, light soup with fufu, banga with starch. The pairings matter. Real pounded yam or fufu beats instant flour.
- **Suya**: at home it's thin-sliced, yaji-coated, grilled over open flame, and served with raw onion and tomato. Export versions can be thick chunks with little yaji.

## Export drift (generic; no named markets)
Milder pepper, paler and wetter jollof, instant-flour swallows, smaller portions of assorted meat, and fewer fermented seasonings (iru, ogiri). Suya cut thick with too little yaji.

## Insider vocabulary (for chef notes)
**Language routing:** Nigerian kitchens get English or Nigerian Pidgin; Pidgin is the safest cross-Nigerian register. Use Yoruba, Igbo or Hausa only when the kitchen's origin is clear, and keep it to a greeting or thanks. Ghanaian kitchens get English (Twi only for thanks). Senegalese kitchens get French, with Wolof only for thanks. Never call a Ghanaian place Nigerian or the reverse.

**Address default:** no address term, or "Chef" / "kitchen team". Use Aunty/Uncle only when the owner's name and gender are known and the user is in-culture. Avoid "my sister / my brother" from Explorers.

## Meal composition norms
One soup or stew plus one swallow per person is a full meal. Jollof plates come with protein (chicken, turkey, beef), plantain (dodo or kelewele) and coleslaw or salad. Add suya or pepper soup as a starter.

## In-culture tells (market-agnostic; market specifics live in `kitchen-scout/markets/`)
Abroad, the kitchens are almost always in-culture. Look for Nigerian vs Ghanaian specialization, real swallows (not instant), and fermented seasonings (iru, ogiri, dawadawa) named on the menu.

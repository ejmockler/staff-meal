# Greek and Cypriot: profile

Scope: Greece and Cyprus. Turkish → turkish.md; Levantine mezze and shawarma → levantine-arab.md. North Macedonian (the Slavic-speaking country) has no profile; it is not "Northern Greece / Macedonia" below.

Desserts and sweet drinks are sweet at home: high for baklava, galaktoboureko, kataifi, loukoumades and spoon sweets (glyko tou koutaliou), med for sweet bougatsa and honey-drizzled pies. Reduce only on the eater's word, and ask for "less syrup" or "less sugar", never none.

## Hard-filter terms (mandatory)
Never infer these from the cuisine. Ask, and record the answer in `dietary:` as confirmed.
- **Pork is the default meat.** In Greece, gyros and souvlaki are most often pork (chicken is the common alternative). Kontosouvli, loukaniko, apaki, louza and Cypriot afelia and loukanika are pork. Greek Cypriot sheftalia is minced pork (sometimes mixed with lamb) wrapped in caul fat, usually pork caul. For pork-free or halal eaters, never assume "lamb" or "chicken" from the menu photo; ask what the meat is.
- **Pork-free ≠ halal.** Chicken gyros from a kitchen that also cooks pork is not halal. Greek kitchens are rarely halal-certified; ask for certification if the eater needs it. Turkish Cypriot kitchens use little pork, which still does not make them halal.
- **Alcohol in cooking**: red wine in stifado, kokkinisto, afelia, loukanika and pastitsada; ouzo in shrimp saganaki and some sauces.
- **Cheese and rennet**: rennet varies by producer. Halloumi is mostly made with microbial or vegetarian rennet; traditional feta, graviera and kefalotyri often use animal rennet, though some large feta makers use vegetarian rennet only. For a vegetarian who avoids animal rennet, ask. PDO feta is at least 70% sheep's milk with up to 30% goat's milk and no cow's milk; feta made outside the EU may contain cow's milk. Cheese hides in horiatiki, pies, saganaki, moussaka and pastitsio.
- **Egg and dairy in sauces**: avgolemono (egg-lemon) thickens soups and giouvarlakia; béchamel has milk, butter and egg; phyllo pies are often brushed with butter.
- **Meats many eaters don't expect (gloss them)**: kokoretsi (lamb offal wrapped in intestines), splinantero, magiritsa (Easter offal soup), patsas (tripe soup), kefalaki (roast lamb's head), gardoumba, chochlioi (snails, Crete), sea urchin (eaten raw), lakerda (cured raw bonito).
- **Nuts, sesame, honey**: walnuts and pistachios in baklava; sesame on koulouri and in tahini (Cyprus); honey in desserts, which matters for vegans.

### Fasting (nistisima): a hard filter, not a preference
Orthodox fasting is a strict rule for many Greek and Greek Cypriot eaters. Record it in `dietary:` with its level, confirmed by the eater.
- **When**: Wednesdays and Fridays in most weeks; Great Lent before Easter; the Nativity fast (15 November to 24 December on the New Calendar used in Greece and Cyprus); the Dormition fast (1–14 August); the Apostles' fast (variable length). Some churches follow the Julian calendar, so dates shift.
- **What a nistisimo (νηστίσιμο) dish excludes**: meat, dairy (cheese, butter, milk, yogurt), eggs and fish with a backbone.
- **What it usually allows**: shellfish, octopus, squid and cuttlefish, fish roe (taramosalata), olive oil and wine on many but not all fast days. Fish depends on the fast: in the Nativity fast (15 Nov–24 Dec on the New Calendar; for Julian-calendar churches the same dates fall on 28 Nov–6 Jan civil) fish is often allowed except on Wednesdays and Fridays for the first part, then dropped for the final stretch; the cutoff varies (Slavic-typikon practice: 20 Dec church calendar = 2 Jan civil for Julian users; some Greek parish guidance drops fish about two weeks before Christmas, and stricter households allow it only on weekends); in Great Lent fish is allowed only on Annunciation and Palm Sunday. Practice varies by church and household, so ask the eater rather than applying a rule.
- **Strict days**: some days, notably in Holy Week and the weekdays of Great Lent, exclude olive oil as well ("without oil", χωρίς λάδι; xerofagia). Most ladera are cooked in oil and fail this.
- **Nistisimo ≠ vegan.** Shellfish and honey are fasting-friendly but not vegan. Vegan food meets the fast except on oil-free days.
- **Hidden non-fasting ingredients**: feta on horiatiki and dakos, butter in phyllo, egg in avgolemono and béchamel, egg wash on pastries, meat stock in rice or giouvetsi, cheese in "vegetable" pies. Spanakopita has feta; a nistisimi spanakopita or hortopita has none.

## Home vs restaurant fidelity markers
- **Gyros vs souvlaki vs döner.** Gyros is meat sliced off a vertical spit; in Greece it is usually stacked pork or chicken slices, not a pressed ground-meat cone. Souvlaki is cubed meat on a skewer (called kalamaki in Athens). In Athens, "souvlaki" also names the whole pita wrap, whatever meat is inside, so name which: "pita gyros" or "pita kalamaki". Döner is the Turkish lineage and belongs in turkish.md; don't route a gyros brief there, and don't call gyros "döner" in a note to a Greek kitchen.
- **Pita wrap**: home = thick, oiled, griddled pita wrapped around the meat with tomato, onion, tzatziki and (in Athens) fried potatoes, often a dusting of paprika. "Me ap' óla" (with everything) means tomato, onion and tzatziki. Export wraps add lettuce, mixed sauces and feta. Northern Greece differs: in Thessaloniki the default dressing is often tomato, onion, mustard and ketchup with fries (inside or on the side; sources disagree), and tzatziki may need asking for (a generalization; shops vary). Name the style rather than assuming the Athens build.
- **Cypriot souvlaki**: the pita is a small pocket, filled rather than wrapped, with salad (tomato, cucumber, parsley, cabbage or onion), lemon, and often sheftalia alongside the souvlaki. Tahini or tzatziki is optional. Souvla is large chunks of lamb or pork on long charcoal spits, a different dish from souvlakia.
- **Horiatiki (Greek salad)**: home = tomato, cucumber, onion, green pepper, olives, a slab of feta on top, dried oregano, olive oil; capers on the islands; no lettuce, no vinaigrette with sugar. Export = lettuce, crumbled cheese, sweet dressing.
- **Tzatziki**: home = thick strained yogurt, plenty of garlic, grated drained cucumber, olive oil. Export = thin, mild, sometimes sweetened.
- **Moussaka and pastitsio**: home = fried or roasted eggplant (moussaka), meat sauce with cinnamon and allspice, a thick set béchamel layer. Export versions skimp on the béchamel or swap it for cheese topping. Cypriot makaronia tou fournou is the local pastitsio.
- **Ladera**: home = vegetables cooked soft in lots of olive oil, often served at room temperature. Al dente or low-oil versions are an outsider's adjustment. Don't ask for "less oil" unless the eater does.
- **Stifado**: home = pearl onions slow-cooked with meat, wine and vinegar until sweet; the sweetness comes from the onions. Ask for "less sweet" only on the eater's word.
- **Greek coffee**: sugar is chosen at order time (skéto, métrio, glykó); all are native. Call it Greek coffee (ελληνικός, ellinikós) in a note to a Greek kitchen, Cypriot coffee to a Cypriot one.
- **Heat**: Greek and Cypriot plates are mild at home, and that is native, not "toned down". Heat lives in specific dishes (tyrokafteri, bourdeto) and in boukovo or pickled peppers on the side. Ask for more only on the eater's word.

## Export drift (generic; no named markets)
Pressed ground beef-and-lamb "gyro" cones instead of stacked slices, lettuce in salads and wraps, crumbled feta substitutes, thin sweetened tzatziki, thin béchamel, al dente low-oil vegetables, over-oreganoed everything, and the menu blended into generic "Mediterranean" with hummus and falafel from the Levant.

## Insider vocabulary (for chef notes)
**Language routing:** Greek (Greek script plus romanization) for Greek and Greek Cypriot kitchens. Cypriot cooks read standard Greek; the Cypriot dialect isn't needed in writing. Turkish Cypriot kitchens: Turkish, using the turkish.md rows. Fallback: English plus dish names in Greek.

**Sensitive pairs:**
- **Greek/Turkish.** Never write a note in Turkish to a Greek or Greek Cypriot kitchen, or in Greek to a Turkish or Turkish Cypriot one. Don't call gyros "döner", tzatziki "cacık", Greek coffee "Turkish coffee", or claim one side's ownership of shared dishes (baklava, dolma/dolmades, kataifi).
- **Cyprus.** Never assume which community a Cypriot kitchen belongs to; read its menu language and dish spellings first. Use "halloumi" or the community's own word (Greek χαλλούμι; Turkish hellim; the EU PDO registered in 2021 reads "Χαλλούμι (Halloumi) / Hellim") and "sheftalia" or "şeftali" to match. Don't label a Cypriot kitchen simply "Greek".
- **Macedonia.** In a Greek kitchen, "Macedonian" means the Greek region. Don't treat it as North Macedonian food, and don't write to it in Macedonian (Slavic).

**Address default:** "Γεια σας" (plural and polite) plus "καλή δουλειά" (good work) needs no address term. "Σεφ" is fine. Avoid "φίλε" (dude), "ρε", "κοπελιά" (girl), and never "μικρέ" or "παιδί μου" (kid, my child), which mark the cook as junior.

## Meal composition norms
Taverna meals are shared. For two: 3–4 mezedes or dips (tzatziki, fava, tyrokafteri, horta), one salad, then one main each or grilled meat by the kilo, with bread always and fried potatoes common. A street meal is 1–2 pitas per person. Cypriot meze is a set run of many small plates, usually ordered for at least two. Fasting tables shift to ladera, fava, octopus, squid and taramosalata. Fruit or a small sweet closes the meal.

## In-culture tells (market-agnostic; market specifics live in `kitchen-scout/markets/`)
- A Greek-script menu and Greek-language reviews.
- Stacked-slice gyros (pork or chicken) and charcoal grills; a pork option is a counter-signal for halal eaters, not a quality flaw.
- Regional dishes (bougatsa, dakos, sofrito, sheftalia, souvla) rather than only "gyro platter".
- Seasonal nistisima menus during Lent and the Nativity fast.
- Greek coffee, tsipouro or ouzo with meze.

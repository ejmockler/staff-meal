## Hard-filter terms
Never infer these. Ask, in the cook's language.
- **Pork is common and often hidden.** Jerk pork, geera pork, griot (pork), pernil/lechón, chicharrón, the Cuban sandwich (pork + ham), mofongo (often with chicharrón), stew peas and red peas soup (pig tail), Bahamian peas 'n' rice (salt pork). Spanish-Caribbean beans, sofrito and rice are often cooked with ham, tocino or lard. Pork-free does not mean halal: many Trini and Guyanese kitchens are Muslim-run and halal, others are not. Ask.
- **"Vegetarian" is not guaranteed.** Callaloo may contain crab or salted pig tail. Saltfish turns up in ackee and in "veg" breakfast plates. Beans may be cooked in meat stock or lard. Doubles and channa are usually vegetarian, but roti can be brushed with butter or ghee. Ital (Rastafarian) food is usually plant-based and often unsalted. Confirm it; the label alone is not enough.
- **Meats to gloss for the eater:** goat (curry goat; it may be mutton), cow foot, tripe (mondongo), mannish water (goat-head soup), souse (pig feet), shark (bake & shark), conch, duck. Guyanese pepperpot traditionally uses pork, cow heel or oxtail. Haitian tasso is usually goat or beef.
- **How to ask:**
  - English: "Does this have any pork or pork fat in it?" / "Is the meat halal?" / "Is there any meat, fish or meat stock in it?"
  - Kreyòl: "Èske gen vyann kochon oswa grès kochon ladan l?" / "Èske vyann lan halal?" / "Èske gen vyann, pwason oswa bouyon vyann ladan l?"
  - Spanish: "¿Esto lleva cerdo o manteca de cerdo?" / "¿La carne es halal?" / "¿Lleva carne, pescado o caldo de carne?"

## Home vs restaurant fidelity markers
- **Jerk**: home = marinated overnight in a scotch bonnet and pimento wet paste, then smoked or grilled over pimento wood; hot, savory and barely sweet. Export = oven-baked chicken with a sweet "jerk sauce". Ask for "cooked the usual way, with the scotch bonnet paste and no sweet sauce." More heat only on the eater's word.
- **Trini pepper is an order-time choice.** "No pepper", "slight pepper", "medium" and "heavy pepper" are the actual counter vocabulary for doubles and roti. Pass along the eater's level. With no word from the eater, "slight pepper" is a common middle choice.
- **Rice & peas** (Jamaican): home = kidney beans ('peas') cooked with coconut milk, thyme, scallion and a whole scotch bonnet; at Christmas they're made with gungo (pigeon) peas. Bahamian peas 'n' rice uses pigeon peas, tomato and salt pork, with no coconut. Neither is plain rice and beans, which is the export substitute.
- **Pikliz**: home = served with every Haitian fried plate. Make sure it comes; ask for extra only on the eater's word.
- **Pepperpot** (Guyanese): home = sweet-savory and dark from cassareep. If it's too sweet, ask for "a little less sweet", never "no sugar".
- **Spanish-Caribbean food isn't chili-hot.** Don't add heat notes. Ask for concrete levers instead: "bien sazonado, con bastante ajo", "tostones bien crujientes, con mojo de ajo"; Puerto Rico: "mofongo con caldo aparte"; Cuba: "con mojo criollo". If a like-home line is wanted: "como en casa, por favor".

## Export drift (generic; no named markets)
Kitchens cooking for outsiders tend to swap smoked jerk for baked chicken with sweet sauce, hold back the scotch bonnet in stews, serve plain rice in place of rice & peas, and treat one island's food as "Caribbean" for all of them.

## Insider vocabulary
**Language routing:** Jamaican, Trinidadian and Guyanese kitchens: English (Patois or Trini creole only when the user is in-culture). Haitian kitchens: Kreyòl, not French and never Spanish. Puerto Rican, Dominican and Cuban kitchens: Spanish. Haitian Kreyòl vs Dominican Spanish is a sensitive pair, so pick by the kitchen's provenance, not the "Caribbean" label. Fallback: English plus dish names.

**Address default:** "Hi Chef", or no address term. Use "Boss" or "Auntie" only when the user is in-culture and the cook is known.

## Meal composition norms
One plate per person (protein, rice & peas, plantain, slaw). Doubles are 2–3 per person as a snack. Roti is one wrap per person, and it's a full meal.

## In-culture tells (market-agnostic)
Abroad, 'Caribbean' kitchens are usually in-culture for ONE island and proxy the rest. Match the island to the dish: Trini/Guyanese for doubles and roti, Jamaican for jerk and patties, Haitian for griot. Look for a real drum or smoker for jerk, pepper ordered by level (slight or heavy), and Kreyòl or Spanish menu text. Who runs these kitchens in a specific market goes in `kitchen-scout/markets/`.

# Market: Singapore and Malaysia

## Local palate vector
High spice literacy, sambal everywhere, and hawker-centre culture. Drift is mostly toward sweeter versions in mall and chain outlets. Heat follows the brief and the dish's own default. Sambal and cut chili usually come on the side here, and that is the home norm, so don't ask for "extra spicy" unless the brief's heat is above the dish's native level. Halal is a hard filter in both directions (see Hard-filter notes).

## Counter and line languages
Singapore: the counter reads English first. Hawker cooks: Mandarin and dialects (Hokkien, Cantonese, Teochew), Malay, Tamil. Mall and chain kitchens often have Malaysian, Filipino, PRC-Chinese or Myanmar staff. Malaysia: the counter reads Malay or English. Chinese kitchens use Cantonese (KL, Ipoh) or Hokkien (Penang). Line cooks are often Bangladeshi, Nepali, Myanmar or Indonesian. Tomyam shops are run by southern-Thai Malay speakers. Short Malay plus English is the safe default for notes in Malaysia.

## Platforms (as of 2026-11; re-verify after 6 months)
- **Singapore:** GrabFood leads, foodpanda is the other major app. **Exit:** Deliveroo closed on 4 March 2026 (DoorDash wound it down), so do not route there.
- **Malaysia:** GrabFood leads, then foodpanda and ShopeeFood. ShopeeFood is often the cheapest and is strongest in the Klang Valley.
- **Both:** Many top hawker stalls are not on any app. Check GrabFood first, then foodpanda (and ShopeeFood in Malaysia), and plan for pickup when a specific stall is the target. foodpanda's owner, Delivery Hero, agreed in July 2026 to be acquired by Uber (closing expected 2H 2027), so re-check before relying on foodpanda field notes.

## Proxy flips
- **Mall-chain hawker dishes:** tuned sweeter, so a poor stand-in for the hawker version. For a hawker brief, order from a hawker stall on the app.
- **Malaysian 'tomyam' shop:** a poor stand-in for central Thai or Isan, but in-culture for southern Thai and Kelantan-style kerabu and Malay-Thai dishes.
- **Mamak shop:** a poor stand-in for Tamil Hindu home food (banana-leaf rice, vegetarian meals), but in-culture for mamak food and, in Penang-origin shops, nasi kandar.
- **Nasi Padang stall (SG/MY):** a poor stand-in for food from Padang itself (milder, sweeter, localised), but in-culture for the Singapore and Malaysian Malay nasi padang tradition.
- **Halal-certified Chinese chain:** a poor stand-in for pork-based Cantonese or Hokkien dishes (char siu, bak kut teh, lard-fried char kway teow), but fine for halal-adapted Malaysian Chinese dishes as Muslim customers know them.
- **Mala xiang guo stall:** a poor stand-in for Sichuan cooking, but mala xiang guo stalls are now a hawker genre of their own in Singapore.

## Hard-filter notes
- **Halal certification:** Malaysia uses JAKIM (and state religious councils) certification. JAKIM-certified halal, Muslim-owned, and 'pork-free' or 'no pork, no lard' (no pork, but may use alcohol or uncertified meat) are distinct, so never treat 'pork-free' as halal. Singapore: look for the MUIS halal logo. An app's halal tag may be merchant-set, so for a strict halal brief, confirm the certificate or logo on the listing or in shop photos.
- **Pork:** easy to find at Chinese hawker stalls, kopitiams and Chinese restaurants in both countries. It is absent from halal-certified chains and from many mall food courts in Malaysia. For a Chinese brief that needs pork and lard, skip halal-certified chains, which substitute chicken or beef and drop the lard.
- **Alcohol in cooking:** Chinese kitchens use Shaoxing or rice wine (drunken chicken, some braises and soups). Halal kitchens don't. Flag this for a no-alcohol brief even when the dish has no pork.
- **Beef:** Hindu-run Indian places often serve no beef. Mamak (Indian-Muslim) shops do.
- **Vegetarian:** Indian vegetarian places (Little India, Brickfields) and Chinese Buddhist vegetarian stalls (often mock meat, sometimes no onion or garlic) are reliable sources.

## Gaps
West African, Ethiopian and Caribbean are thin. Regional Mexican is thin.

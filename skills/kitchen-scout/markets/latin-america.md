# Market: Latin America (Mexico, Brazil, wider region; approximately)

## Local palate vector
- **Mexico**: foreign food gets chile, lime and salsa added. Asian food gets chipotle/spicy mayo, sweet 'salsa de anguila', Valentina or Tajín, and breaded (empanizado) treatments; sushi gets queso Philadelphia and spicy mayo.
- **Brazil**: foreign food gets cream cheese (sushi "hot roll"), catupiry, and sweeter sauces. Spice tolerance is low to moderate by default, so foreign dishes are often cooked milder than at home.
- Heat: undo the local drift in either direction. In Mexico, ask for added chile, Tajín or spicy mayo to be left off when the home version has none. In Brazil, ask for native heat when the kitchen has toned it down. Ask for more than the dish's native heat only when the brief's heat is above it.

## Counter and line languages
Spanish at the counter (Portuguese in Brazil). On the line: in São Paulo, often nordestino Brazilians whatever the cuisine (including sushi counters); in Santiago, Lima and Bogotá, many Venezuelan cooks; in Chile, also Haitian (Kreyòl) and Peruvian staff; in Buenos Aires, Bolivian, Paraguayan and Peruvian cooks. Diaspora communities with their own kitchens: Japanese-Brazilian (Nikkei), Syrian-Lebanese, Korean, Chinese (São Paulo pastelarias are largely Chinese-run), Peruvian, Venezuelan. A plain Spanish/Portuguese line is the safe default; add the cuisine's language only for owner-run diaspora kitchens.

## Proxy flips
1. **Peruvian chifa**: a poor stand-in for Cantonese, but in-culture for chifa (chaufa, tallarín saltado, aeropuerto).
2. **Peruvian Nikkei bars**: a poor stand-in for traditional Japanese, but in-culture for Nikkei (tiradito, acevichado maki).
3. **São Paulo pastelarias (Chinese-run)**: a poor stand-in for Chinese food, but in-culture for Brazilian pastel de feira with caldo de cana.
4. **Brazilian temakerias / sushi rodízio**: a poor stand-in for Japanese, but in-culture for Brazilian-style sushi (temaki, hot roll) when the brief asks for that.
5. **Puebla taquerías árabes**: a poor stand-in for Levantine shawarma, but in-culture for tacos árabes (pan árabe, chipotle salsa).
6. **Mexicali "comida china"**: a poor stand-in for Cantonese, but in-culture for Mexicali's Chinese-Mexican tradition.
7. **Brazilian esfiharias (chains like Habib's)**: a poor stand-in for Lebanese, but in-culture for Brazilian open-faced esfiha.

## Hard-filter notes
- **Pork**: common everywhere. In Mexico it shows up as al pastor, carnitas and cochinita pibil, and as lard (manteca) in tamales, refried beans, flour tortillas and pan dulce. In Brazil it shows up in feijoada, linguiça, bacon in farofa and torresmo. For no-pork orders ask "sin cerdo ni manteca de cerdo" / "sem carne de porco nem banha".
- **Halal**: there is no state certification. Private religious bodies certify mainly export meat (in Brazil, FAMBRAS Halal and CDIAL Halal), and certified restaurants are rare. Most Syrian-Lebanese diaspora restaurants are Christian-run, so "Lebanese" does not mean halal. Look for mosque-adjacent places (Brás in São Paulo, Foz do Iguaçu) and ask directly.
- **Alcohol**: cooking with beer, wine or cachaça is common (frijoles charros, pollo a la cerveza, some moquecas and stews). In Mexico, election-day dry laws (ley seca) can block alcohol sales on apps, and alcohol delivery is restricted by hour in some places.
- **Labeling**: front-of-pack warnings apply to packaged food, not restaurant menus (Mexico's NOM-051 black octagons, Chile's Ley 20.606 stop signs, Brazil's ANVISA magnifying glass). Menus rarely list allergens. "Vegetariano" can still mean chicken stock or lard, so spell it out: "sin caldo de pollo ni manteca".

## Gaps
South Asian, West African and Ethiopian cuisines are thin across most of the region.

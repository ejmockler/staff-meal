# Market: Europe (continental)

## Local palate vector
Milder overall, especially in Germany, the Nordics, Spain and Italy. Concrete drifts: Germany adds cream and fruit (mango, pineapple or banana in curries, sauce on everything); France adds cheese (naan fromage, cheese sauce in tacos) and sweetens; the Netherlands runs sweet (ketjap) but tolerates sambal on the side; Spain and Italy have a low heat ceiling ('picante'/'piccante' is about 2–3). 'Scharf' (German) or 'épicé' (French) on a menu means about 3–4 on the heat ladder. Push heat only up to the brief's level, which defaults to the dish's home norm. When that is above what the menu word implies, write 'bitte so scharf, wie Sie es selbst essen' or 'épicé comme vous le mangez vous-même, s'il vous plaît'. These phrases ask for the kitchen's own home level, not the maximum.

## Counter and line languages
Counter: the local language first. English works in NL, Germany and the Nordics but is unreliable in France, Spain and Italy, so put the local language on line 1 there. Line languages vary by kitchen: Turkish or Kurdish (döner/kebab), Arabic (Levantine, Maghrebi, Egyptian), Urdu/Punjabi (Pakistani), Bengali (Bangladeshi kitchens in Spain, Italy, Portugal), Nepali (Finland, Portugal, Germany), Dari/Pashto (Afghan kitchens in Germany), Tamil (Paris, Germany), Vietnamese (German 'Asia'/sushi, Czechia, Poland), Mandarin/Wenzhounese (French and Italian sushi and Asian kitchens).

## Proxy flips
- German "Indian" run by Pakistanis or Afghans: order the Pakistani karahi or the Afghan dishes, not the Indian menu.
- Paris "Indien" run by Tamils: order the Sri Lankan dishes (kottu, string hoppers).
- German "Asia / Sushi / Thai" run by Vietnamese: order the Vietnamese section (phở, bún bò Huế, bánh cuốn), not the sushi or Thai curry.
- Finnish (and Portuguese, German) "Indian" run by Nepalis: order the Nepali section (momo, thali).
- Madrid "Indio" and Rome "Indiano" run by Bangladeshis: order the Bangladeshi dishes (kacchi biryani, fish bhuna).
- Swedish pizzeria / kebab run by Kurds, Assyrians or Arabs: look for the Kurdish or Levantine dishes on an Arabic or Kurdish menu.
- Paris 13e "Vietnamese" run by Teochew families from Cambodia or Laos: in-culture for Teochew and Cambodian noodle soups (hủ tiếu / kuy teav), not only phở.

## Hard-filter notes
- **Pork:** common in German, Central European, Spanish (jamón, chorizo, lard) and Italian cooking. Döner, shoarma and kebab from Turkish, Arab or Kurdish shops are normally chicken, veal, beef or lamb. Greek gyros is usually pork, both in Greece and at German Greek restaurants. Chinees-Indisch babi dishes are pork.
- **Halal:** there is no state certification. Most halal claims are self-declared signs, and private certifiers vary by country. A halal sign covers the meat, not the whole kitchen. Ask before ordering if cross-contact or alcohol matters.
- **Alcohol:** wine, beer or spirits are normal in French, Italian, German and Greek cooking (Metaxa-Sauce, coq au vin, beer batter). Turkish and Arab-run shops may still sell alcohol.
- **Labeling:** EU Regulation 1169/2011 requires disclosure of 14 allergens, including for non-prepacked restaurant food. On apps this often appears only as "ask the restaurant" or an allergen link. 'Vegetarisch'/'végétarien' items may still use fish sauce or meat stock in Asian kitchens.

## Gaps
North African cuisine is huge in France (and in Belgium, Spain and the Netherlands) (profile: `taste-brief/cuisines/maghrebi.md`). Regional Mexican is thin except in Madrid and Barcelona (real taquerías). Caribbean is thin outside the Netherlands (Surinamese, Antillean in Rotterdam), France (Antillean) and Spain (Dominican, Cuban in Madrid). Japanese-run Japanese is concentrated in Düsseldorf and Paris rue Sainte-Anne; elsewhere expect Chinese- or Vietnamese-run sushi. Regional Chinese (Sichuan, Dongbei) is reliable mainly in Paris, Berlin, Amsterdam, Milan and Madrid (Usera).
